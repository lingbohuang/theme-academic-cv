1. Simple Theoretical Framework

Red text marks substantive edits or additions relative to the current appendix.

This section extends the model in the main text from a binary “give versus give zero” decision to a setting in which the amount of out-of-pocket giving can vary. The extension shows that the norm-signalling mechanism generates the same qualitative prediction for both the frequency of giving and the amount given: a match increases giving when the ask cannot be avoided, but has no effect on giving in the Avoid condition in the norm-signalling benchmark.

Consider an individual endowed with wealth \(w\), which can be allocated between private consumption and an out-of-pocket donation \(g \in [0,w]\). The individual’s utility has two components. The first component is consumption and altruistic utility, \[ v(g;a_i)=w-g+a_i u(g), \] where \(a_i \geq 0\) measures how much individual \(i\) cares about the charity, \(u(0)=0\), \(u'(g)>0\), and \(u''(g)<0\). In this benchmark, \(g\) is the donor’s out-of-pocket gift. The model deliberately shuts down the standard price channel so that the match can affect behavior only through the norm-signalling channel.

The second component is the psychological cost of falling short of the norm of giving. Let \(g^\ell \in (0,w]\) denote the norm level: gifts at or above \(g^\ell\) fully satisfy the norm. When the individual faces the ask, the psychological cost is \[ h_m(g;H_i)=\kappa_m H_i (g^\ell-g)_+, \] where \((z)_+=\max\{z,0\}\), \(H_i \geq 0\) is the individual’s sensitivity to the norm, \(\kappa_0=1\) in the No Match condition, and \(\kappa_1=\gamma>1\) in the Match condition. Thus, the match raises the psychological cost of giving less than the norm only when the ask is faced. This is the formal content of the norm-signalling mechanism.

If the individual avoids the ask, no donation decision is presented and the individual receives \(v(0;a_i)=w\). If the individual does not avoid the ask, utility from giving \(g\) in match condition \(m\in\{0,1\}\) is \[ U_m(g;a_i,H_i)=w-g+a_i u(g)-\kappa_m H_i(g^\ell-g)_+. \] The timing is therefore simple. In the No Avoid condition, the individual chooses \(g\). In the Avoid condition, the individual first chooses whether to avoid the ask; conditional on not avoiding, the individual chooses \(g\). We solve the problem by backward induction.

Two modelling choices keep the appendix parallel to the main text. First, the appendix is a norm-signalling benchmark: \(m\) does not enter \(v(g;a_i)\). Second, to make the three-type classification exhaustive, assume that individuals who are not pure givers would give zero absent the psychological cost of violating the norm. Formally, for every individual either \(a_i u'(g^\ell)>1\), or \(a_i u'(0)\leq 1\). This rules out intrinsic small donors who would give a positive amount below \(g^\ell\) even with \(H_i=0\). Allowing such donors would not change the main Avoid versus No Avoid prediction for the frequency of giving; they can be grouped with always-givers for that purpose.

Baseline Giving and Type Classification

We first solve the No Avoid - No Match treatment. If \(g<g^\ell\), the first-order condition is \[ a_i u'(g)+H_i=1. \] If \(g\geq g^\ell\), the psychological cost is zero and the marginal condition is \(a_i u'(g)=1\). Hence an individual gives more than the norm level if \[ a_i u'(g^\ell)>1. \] These individuals are pure givers: they care enough about the charity to give more than \(g^\ell\) even without psychological pressure.

For all other individuals, \(a_i u'(0)\leq 1\). Define two thresholds: \[ H_i^L=1-a_i u'(0), \qquad H_i^U=1-a_i u'(g^\ell). \] Since \(u'\) is decreasing, \(0\leq H_i^L<H_i^U\). We ignore knife-edge indifference cases, or equivalently assume that ties are resolved toward the lower gift and toward avoiding the ask.

The remaining individuals fall into two groups.

  • Pure givers satisfy \(a_i u'(g^\ell)>1\). They give \(g_i^*>g^\ell\) in the No Avoid - No Match treatment.
  • Reluctant givers satisfy \(H_i^L < H_i \leq H_i^U\). They do not care enough about the charity to give absent the norm, but the psychological cost of giving less than the norm induces them to give a positive amount \(0<g_i^*\leq g^\ell\).
  • Non-givers satisfy \(H_i\leq H_i^L\). Their norm cost is too small to induce giving, so they give zero in the No Avoid - No Match treatment.

This classification preserves the intuition in the main text while making the threshold argument explicit. Pure givers give because of altruistic utility. Reluctant givers give because the norm cost makes giving zero unattractive. Non-givers give zero because both altruism and norm pressure are too weak.

Matching When the Ask Cannot Be Avoided

In the No Avoid - Match treatment, the match raises the psychological cost of giving less than \(g^\ell\) from \(H_i(g^\ell-g)_+\) to \(\gamma H_i(g^\ell-g)_+\). For gifts below the norm, the first-order condition becomes \[ a_i u'(g)+\gamma H_i=1. \] The relevant thresholds under the match are \[ H_i^{LM}=\frac{1-a_i u'(0)}{\gamma}=\frac{H_i^L}{\gamma}, \qquad H_i^{UM}=\frac{1-a_i u'(g^\ell)}{\gamma}=\frac{H_i^U}{\gamma}. \] Because \(\gamma>1\), both thresholds are lower under the match.

For reluctant givers, matching weakly increases the amount given. If \(H_i>H_i^{UM}\), the reluctant giver is pushed to the norm and gives \(g^\ell\). If \(H_i^L < H_i\leq H_i^{UM}\), the giver remains below the norm but gives more than in the No Match condition.

For non-givers, matching can increase the frequency of giving. Non-givers with \(H_i^{LM} < H_i\leq H_i^L\) switch from giving zero to giving a positive amount. Non-givers with \(H_i\leq H_i^{LM}\) continue to give zero. Thus, the match raises the frequency of giving only if there is positive mass of non-givers close enough to the giving margin. It raises out-of-pocket giving through two margins: reluctant givers give more, and marginal non-givers begin to give.

Matching When the Ask Can Be Avoided

In the Avoid condition, the individual can avoid the ask and receive \(w\). Pure givers still choose to give because \(a_i u'(g^\ell)>1\) implies that their preferred gift gives utility above \(w\). Reluctant givers and non-givers, however, weakly prefer avoiding the ask to facing it.

For these individuals, \(a_i u'(0)\leq 1\), so absent the psychological cost they do not have an intrinsic reason to give. If they avoid the ask, they receive \(w\) and incur no psychological cost. If they do not avoid, the best they can do is no better than \(w\), and usually strictly worse because they either give a costly positive amount or explicitly give zero while facing the psychological cost. The statement is weak rather than strict because non-givers with \(H_i=0\) may be indifferent between avoiding and explicitly giving zero. This tie does not affect effective giving, but it matters for claims about the frequency of skipping.

Therefore, reluctant givers and non-givers give zero in the Avoid condition, regardless of whether a match is present. The match has no effect on their effective giving because avoiding the ask removes the psychological cost to which the match would otherwise add force.

The following proposition summarizes the mechanism.

Proposition S1. In the norm-signalling benchmark, matching weakly increases both the frequency of giving and average out-of-pocket giving in the No Avoid condition. The increase is strict if there is positive mass of non-givers with \(H_i^{LM} < H_i\leq H_i^L\) or reluctant givers whose giving rises under the match. In the Avoid condition, matching has no effect on effective giving by reluctant givers or non-givers, because they can avoid the ask and eliminate the psychological cost of not giving.

The proposition also implies the frequency ranking used in the main text: \[ \text{No Avoid - Match} \geq \text{No Avoid - No Match} \geq \text{Avoid - Match} = \text{Avoid - No Match}. \]

Relation to the Standard Price Channel

The benchmark above abstracts from the effective price mechanism. If one wants to include that channel, the altruistic part of utility should depend on the total amount received by the charity rather than only on the donor’s out-of-pocket gift. For example, with a one-for-one match, the first utility component could be written as \[ v_m(g;a_i)=w-g+a_i u((1+m)g). \] In that hybrid model, the match can increase giving even in the Avoid condition because a dollar of out-of-pocket giving produces more charitable output. The norm-signalling prediction would then be less extreme: the match should have a larger effect when the ask cannot be avoided than when it can be avoided, rather than exactly zero effect in the Avoid condition. The experimental results suggest that the norm-signalling channel dominates the standard price channel in this setting.
